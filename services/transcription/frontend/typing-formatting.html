Typing & Formatting

Formatting is part of accuracy

Good formatting makes speech easier to follow without changing what the speaker meant.

Lesson 01 · Foundation

One recording, several valid formats

The right format is the one requested by the client or project style guide. Do not assume every job wants the same transcript. A verbatim transcript preserves speech features; clean verbatim removes selected clutter according to the rules; intelligent or edited transcription prioritizes readable wording while preserving the original meaning.

Speaker labels and timestamps are usually layout requirements that can be combined with a transcription style. For example, a transcript can be clean verbatim and speaker-labeled, or verbatim and timestamped.

💡
Prime rule

Follow the job instructions first. If a rule is not specified, use a consistent, readable style and do not invent missing words, speaker identities, or timestamps.

Lesson 02 · Format library

Five core transcript formats

Select a format to inspect its rules and example. The sample dialogue below is illustrative training content, not a claim that every client uses identical conventions.

01 · Verbatim formattingPRESERVE SPEECH DETAILS
Speaker 1: Um, I, I thought we were meeting at, uh, nine, but—
Speaker 2: No, we said nine-thirty.
Speaker 1: Right, right. Sorry about that.

Verbatim captures the words as spoken, including repetitions, filler words, false starts, and meaningful interruptions, according to the requested convention.

Keep what is heard

Do not silently repair grammar or replace a speaker’s words with what you think they intended.

Use consistent punctuation

Represent pauses, interruptions, and unfinished speech according to the project guide.

Do not guess

If a word is unclear, replay it and use the prescribed unclear-audio notation if necessary.

Respect the specification

Some projects define strict rules for fillers, stutters, non-speech sounds, and false starts.

Lesson 03 · One speaker

Single-speaker transcription and paragraphing

For a recording with one speaker, you usually do not need a speaker label on every paragraph unless the instructions request it. The priority is consistent paragraphing and accurate identification of the speaker throughout the recording.

  • Start a new paragraph when the speaker begins a new topic, completes a major thought, or moves into a distinct section.
  • Keep paragraphs readable. Avoid one enormous wall of text, but do not split every sentence into its own paragraph without a reason.
  • Keep headings, timestamps, and labels in the same position and style throughout.
  • If another voice briefly appears, do not automatically assume it is the main speaker. Label it only when the voice can be identified confidently or use the project’s neutral speaker label.
Single speaker · consistent paragraphsILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE
The meeting will begin at ten o’clock. Please have the updated report ready before we start.

We will review the budget first, then discuss the delivery schedule. If anything has changed, let the team know before the meeting.
📝
Paragraphing rule

Paragraph breaks improve readability, but they must not change the order or meaning of the spoken content.

Lesson 04 · Multiple speakers

Identify speakers consistently

When several people speak, use the labels required by the project. If names are known and the assignment permits names, use them consistently. Otherwise, neutral labels such as Speaker 1 and Speaker 2 are safer than guessing.

  • Assign each voice a stable label when it first appears.
  • Do not switch between “Speaker 1,” “Man 1,” and a guessed name for the same voice.
  • Start a new paragraph when the speaker changes.
  • For overlapping speech, follow the project’s overlap notation. Do not pretend you can confidently hear words that are masked.
  • For court hearings, interviews, or meetings, preserve the roles and speaker turns accurately. Do not infer legal titles or identities beyond the source.
Multiple speakers · label each turnILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE
Speaker 1: We are starting the review now.

Speaker 2: I have the updated document ready.

Speaker 1: Thank you. Please share it with the team.
Lesson 05 · Time markers

Timestamped formatting

Timestamps help readers locate a word, speaker turn, or section in the source recording. The required frequency varies by project: every 30 seconds, every speaker change, at intervals, or at specific events. Never invent a timestamp based on how long a sentence looks.

Interval style

[00:00:30] Speaker 1: We can begin now.

[00:01:00] Speaker 2: I’m ready.

Use the requested interval and time format consistently.

Speaker-turn range

[00:00:30–00:00:42]
Speaker 1: We can begin now.

[00:00:42–00:00:51]
Speaker 2: I’m ready.

Use start/end ranges only when requested or supported by the workflow.

⏱️
Time format

Use the format in the instructions, for example HH:MM:SS. Confirm whether timestamps refer to the start of a turn, an interval, or both.

Final lesson · Quality control
Formatting checklist


  • Did I follow the exact requested transcript style?
  • Are filler words, repetitions, false starts, and grammar edits handled according to that style?
  • Are speaker labels stable and attached to the correct voice?
  • Are paragraph breaks logical, consistent, and readable?
  • Are timestamps in the correct format and tied to the source audio?
  • Did I preserve names, dates, numbers, and the meaning of the original speech?
  • Did I mark unclear audio only after replaying it?
  • Did I protect confidential recordings and follow the client’s handling instructions?
